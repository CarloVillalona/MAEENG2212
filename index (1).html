MAEENG 2212 — Methodologies of Teaching Literature
Section 2-MAED-ENG-Z  |  Master of Arts in Education, Major in English  |  1st Semester, SY 2026–2027

Course Overview

A graduate seminar-workshop in the theory, research, and practice of teaching literature in English-language classrooms — from foundational purposes and reader-response theory through culturally responsive, critical, and digital/multimodal approaches, culminating in a theoretically grounded literature-teaching unit.

Course Type

Major / Professional Education

Delivery Mode

Online Blended — Synchronous & Asynchronous

Total Learning Time

64 hours over 4 weeks (12h synchronous, 20h independent reading, 32h asynchronous)

Course Description

This course examines major approaches to literary pedagogy — reader-response, language-based, stylistic, critical, cultural, transactional, aesthetic, and sociocultural — and how these approaches shape text selection, classroom interaction, learning activities, assessment, and curricular decisions. It addresses the teaching of fiction, poetry, drama, young adult literature, Philippine literature, world literature, and multimodal/digital texts, with attention to critical literacy, culturally responsive teaching, inclusive literary education, and technology. Students engage with scholarly literature, evaluate competing approaches, design literature-based learning experiences, conduct microteaching, and develop a theoretically justified literature-teaching unit for a specific educational context.

Task 1 · Week 1 — Critical Literature Pedagogy Reflection #1 50 pts

▾

"What Ordinary Reading Instruction Cannot Do"

Word limit: 800–1,000 words (written component; quotations, artifacts, and references additional)  ·  Due: Sunday of Week 1, 8:00 PM — posted to the Week 1 Discussion channel, with two substantive peer replies

Identify one real learner and have an actual conversation this week about a short literary text they have read recently. Capture at least one direct quotation (identified only by an appropriate pseudonym and general description). In 800–1,000 words, open with that quotation and argue a defensible position on the guiding question “What should literature education accomplish that ordinary reading instruction cannot?” using at least two theoretical frames from Week 1. Connect explicitly to the Lesson A / Lesson B distinction from the comparative-lesson workshop, and close by revising or defending your one-sentence exit-reflection answer from the session.

This is deliberately not an essay completable from theory alone. A response with a strong argument but no real conversation behind it, or a suspiciously generic quotation such as "reading helps me understand life," will not meet the evidentiary standard this task requires.

Read Before You Write

  • Required — Pajares, M. F. (1992). Teachers' beliefs and educational research: Cleaning up a messy construct. Review of Educational Research, 62(3), 307–332. Open-access PDF
  • Recommended — Rosenblatt, L. M. Literature as Exploration. Free to borrow, Internet Archive

Task 2 · Week 2 — Approach-to-Practice Analysis 50 pts

▾

"One Text, Three Pedagogies, One Real Reaction"

Word limit: 800–1,000 words  ·  Due: Sunday of Week 2, 8:00 PM — posted to the Week 2 Discussion channel, with two substantive peer replies

Choose one literary text you already teach, plan to teach, or could plausibly teach. Design three genuinely different short teaching sequences for that text, each built on a distinct pedagogical approach from Week 2 (information-based, language-based, reader-response, stylistic, cultural, personal-growth, critical literacy, or aesthetic/exploratory) — specifying teacher role, learner role, central questions, and evidence of learning for each. Then actually try one of the three with real learners this week (a class, a small group, a single learner, or a colleague role-playing genuinely) and record at least one real, near-verbatim response.

In 800–1,000 words, evaluate all three approaches, giving special weight to the one you actually tried: what happened that you expected, what didn't, and what that reveals about the approach's strengths and limitations for this text and this group. Use theory to explain any gap between prediction and observation — start by asking whether the real reaction reflects an efferent or aesthetic stance, and whether the pedagogy invited or worked against that stance. Close by stating which approach you would actually choose going forward, and why.

A response that only theorizes three approaches without a genuinely attempted trial and a real, specific reaction will be graded as incomplete on the evidentiary criterion below, regardless of theoretical polish.

  • Recommended — Widdowson, H. G. Stylistics and the Teaching of Literature. Free to borrow, Internet Archive

Task 3 · Week 3 — Critical Text Selection Portfolio 50 pts

▾

"Whose Voice Is in the Room?"

Word limit: 800–1,000 words  ·  Due: Sunday of Week 3, 8:00 PM — posted to the Week 3 Discussion channel, with two substantive peer replies

Select a literary text currently used, or considered for use, in your own teaching context (or a program you know well). First, conduct a critical text audit: whose voice is represented? Whose is absent? What identities or assumptions does the text present as default? What linguistic, cultural, or developmental demands does it place on your actual learners? Ground this in at least one quoted passage. Second, talk this week to at least two real learners or colleagues about their actual reaction to the text (or a similar one), recording at least one direct quotation from each, using pseudonyms.

In 800–1,000 words, write a Literature Text Rationale arguing for keeping, replacing, or reframing this text, using the syllabus's own selection grounds: literary value, pedagogical value, learner appropriateness, cultural representation, linguistic demand, and ethical considerations. Your rationale must directly engage what your two real respondents told you — agreeing with, complicating, or pushing back against your own audit using their actual words. Identify one genuine anticipated challenge and one concrete way you would address it.

A rationale built only on your own inference about "what students would probably think" does not meet the standard — the point of this week's audit is testing your assumptions against actual readers.

Read Before You Write
  • Required — Rudine Sims Bishop (1990). "Mirrors, Windows, and Sliding Glass Doors" (PDF)
  • Required — "Multicultural Education: Development, Dimensions, and Challenges" (Univ. of Washington, summarizing James A. Banks)
  • Recommended — José Rizal, Noli Me Tangere / El Filibusterismo. Free download, Project Gutenberg

Task 4 · Week 4 — Reflective Commentary: Formative Check-In 50 pts

▾

"Piloting One Piece of Your Unit"

Word limit: 800–1,000 words  ·  Due: Sunday of Week 4, 8:00 PM — posted to the Week 4 Discussion channel, with two substantive peer replies

This Sunday task feeds into the Week 4 Reflective Commentary component of the Theory-to-Practice Literature Teaching Unit (the major final performance task — graded separately at 25% for the unit and 10% for the reflective commentary; see Overview → Assessment Plan). It is a smaller, discrete formative piece, not the final unit submission itself.

Select one activity, question sequence, or assessment moment from your in-progress teaching unit — something specific enough to run in 15–25 minutes. Pilot that one piece this week with real learners (your class, a volunteer group, a colleague's class, or peers/family standing in as readers, run genuinely rather than performed). Collect one real, specific artifact: learner writing, a transcribed exchange, a de-identified photo of a worksheet, or a close paraphrase including at least one direct quotation.

In 800–1,000 words, write a focused reflective commentary addressing four things: what you expected the activity to produce, based on your unit's theoretical framework; what the pilot actually produced, using your artifact/quotation as primary evidence; where theory and reality diverged, and your best explanation why; and exactly what you will revise in your final unit as a result — a specific, describable change, not a vague intention to "improve engagement."

Read Before You Write
  • Required — Bloom's Taxonomy of Educational Objectives (PDF, IES / Northeast REL, U.S. Dept. of Education)
  • Recommended — Revisit Pajares (1992) from Week 1. Open-access PDF
